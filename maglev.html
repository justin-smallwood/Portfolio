Electromagnetic Levitation and Propulsion for Model Trains

My senior thesis for the Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering at Princeton University investigates how permanent magnets and electromagnetic coils can be coordinated to control the movement of a levitating model train. Using microcontrollers, transistors, and Time-of-Flight (ToF) distance sensors, the project develops a methodology for designing and building a magnetic levitation (maglev) train model inspired by real-world EDS maglev systems used in high-speed rail across Europe and Asia. The thesis was completed as part of MAE 498 and 499 (Senior Independent Work), advised by Professor Craig Arnold.

Background

Maglev trains offer significant societal benefits through faster travel, sustainable infrastructure, and opportunities for economic expansion. These trains can exceed 310 mph due to the absence of rail friction. There are two main classifications of electromagnetic suspension: Electromagnetic Suspension (EMS), which uses attractive forces, and Electrodynamic Suspension (EDS), which uses repulsive forces. This thesis focuses on modeling an EDS system, in which the train levitates above a U-shaped guideway using repulsive permanent magnets, stabilized and propelled by side-mounted electromagnets driven by a direct current power supply.

The primary design inspiration came from a YouTube project by Hyperspace Pirate, in which permanent magnets and evenly spaced solenoids propel a 3D-printed train around a circular track. This thesis extended that concept by attempting to eliminate all physical contact between the train and the track, requiring additional horizontal electromagnetic stabilization.

Design Requirements

Three core design requirements were established for the system:

  • Vertical Levitation: The train must not contact the track bottom or exceed the height of the guide rails.
  • Horizontal Stability: The train must fit inside the straight and curved segments of the track with adequate side clearance for movement.
  • Scalability: The code must be scalable to support the addition of more track segments.

Two design iterations were evaluated across seven requirements covering heat generation, permanent magnet stability, electromagnet stability, individual segment control, sensor integration, and scalability of both the circuit and the physical design.

Train and Track Design

The model train and track were designed entirely in PTC Creo. The train measures 6 in. x 2 in. x 2 in. and features a curved nose inspired by the Japanese SCMaglev to reduce aerodynamic drag. A rear hook allows additional train cars to be coupled to the lead unit. The entire track is 40 in. x 24 in. The U-shaped track has an inner diameter of 4 inches and is divided into 8-inch segments connected by L-shaped interlocking joints with a nominal clearance of 0.01 in. to accommodate 3D printing tolerances.

MATLAB code was written to calculate the minimum track width required for the train to traverse curved segments without contacting the guide rails. Grooves beneath each track segment accommodate wiring, and holes at the midpoint of each segment route magnet wires along the guide rails. Permanent magnets are mounted along the bottom of the track for levitation, while electromagnet supports hook over the guide rails on both sides for stability and propulsion.

Engineering drawings were produced for the interlocking track joints to create a tight fit, ensuring accurate tolerancing for 3D-printed assembly.

Permanent Magnet Testing

Two sizes of neodymium magnets were tested. Smaller 0.25 in. diameter grade N35 magnets (1.2 lbf pull force) were evaluated in several configurations, including same-pole orientation and Halbach arrays. Their localized magnetic fields resulted in limited levitation range and positional instability.

Larger 1.25 in. diameter grade N30 magnets (18 lbf pull force) performed significantly better due to their broader, more spatially extensive magnetic fields. Two track configurations were tested: a paired arrangement of two magnets and an X-shaped configuration of five magnets. The X-shape used the fewest magnets while minimizing gaps in field coverage, and was selected for the final design.

Circuit and Control System

The final circuit design consisted of three VL53L0X Time-of-Flight ToF sensors and six MOSFET modules, with each module driving two electromagnets. Flyback diodes were connected across each MOSFET module to protect the circuit from high-voltage inductive kickback when the electromagnets were switched off. The electromagnet sets were evenly spaced across two straight sections of the track.

An Arduino Nano read ToF sensor data directly via I2C, with each sensor assigned a unique address using XSHUT pins. The code implemented three duty cycle levels (50%, 90%, and 100%) to vary electromagnetic field strength based on the train's real-time position. Pressing 'R' or 'L' on a keyboard triggered directional propulsion by boosting the duty cycle of the opposing electromagnet set to 100%.

Electromagnet Design

Two magnet wire gauges were evaluated for the electromagnet coils. In the first design iteration, 30 AWG wire was used because its smaller diameter allowed a denser coil with more turns per unit length, increasing magnetic field strength. However, the high resistance of 30 AWG wire produced excessive heat, and after approximately 2 seconds of operation the wire emitted smoke and caused deformation of the PLA electromagnet support. Reduced duty cycles (down to 35%) prevented overheating but produced insufficient field strength for stabilization.

The second iteration used 20 AWG wire, which showed no signs of thermal failure at a 100% duty cycle and 5 A maximum current. Although the thicker wire limited the number of turns that could fit in the support, a comparable magnetic field strength was achieved. The target horizontal force was calculated using the magnetomotive force equation, with a safety factor of 3 applied to compensate for duty cycle variation. The final design used 50 turns of 20 AWG wire per coil.

Results

Although a fully levitating prototype was not realized, the project yielded valuable insights. The 20 AWG electromagnets met the heat generation requirement but did not produce sufficient field strength to stabilize the train at the center of the track. The permanent magnets similarly could not achieve stable vertical levitation, as the system depended on the horizontal electromagnets first positioning the train at the track centerline.

The sensor and control system performed as intended throughout testing. The Arduino successfully read real-time positional changes from the ToF sensors and updated the surrounding electromagnet duty cycles accordingly. Common points of failure were in the corners of the track and in regions of weak magnetic field between permanent magnet pairs.

Future Work

Several directions were identified for future iterations of this project:

  • Narrowing the track width to bring the train closer to the side electromagnets and increase interaction with the horizontal magnetic fields.
  • Replacing circular permanent track magnets with square magnets to reduce dead spots in the magnetic field coverage.
  • Incorporating a soft iron core to increase electromagnet strength, or sourcing a DC power supply capable of exceeding 5 A.
  • Mounting electromagnets on the floor of the track and integrating Hall effect sensors for real-time vertical stabilization.
  • Exploring an EMS maglev configuration or an AC power supply to more closely replicate conventional maglev operating systems.
